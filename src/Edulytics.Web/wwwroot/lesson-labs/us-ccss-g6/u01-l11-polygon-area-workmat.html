11.2 — Identify polygons precisely

For every figure, decide whether its boundary is a single, closed, non-self-crossing chain of straight segments. An inward corner does not disqualify a polygon; curved corners, missing joins and a whole three-dimensional object do.

Original practice examples, described in words: A closed straight arrow; B rectangle-like with rounded corners; C closed straight-edged star; D a triangle with a gap; E curved heart; F a 3D rectangular prism. Explain why A and C are plane polygons, and why the others are not.

Teacher: ask students to distinguish a polygon's boundary from the region whose area is measured. The number check below does not assess this explanation.

Original 11.4 source-grid geometry — area by dissection

Use the grid to split the original-shape region into a central tilted square and four triangles. The central area is 20 square units, and each of the four wings is 5 square units. Explain how you know the pieces cover the region without overlap. This is NOT the separately labeled first-party 12-unit sample in the lesson notes.

Teacher check: the source-area sum is 20 + 5 + 5 + 5 + 5 = 40 square units. The square can be proved by enclosure/subtraction, not by assuming an irrational diagonal has an integer length.

Short source-practice numerical check

Edulytics independent Cool-down — not publisher 11.5

Draw and decompose a trapezoid with parallel bases 10 cm and 6 cm and perpendicular height 4 cm. Find its area, show two different plans, and explain why two matching trapezoids can form a parallelogram. The correct area is 32 cm².

Teacher rubric: A valid closed four-sided trapezoid with parallel bases; a 90° perpendicular height of 4; a decomposition into triangles and rectangle or into two congruent copies; correct 32 cm² with units; a written explanation. The number checker cannot certify the drawing or reasoning.
