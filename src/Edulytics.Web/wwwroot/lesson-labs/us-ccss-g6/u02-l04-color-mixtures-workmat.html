Color mixtures: same ratio, same predicted shade

Use independently authored numeric models; they are not a prediction of exact physical shade under different pigments or lighting. For students with color-vision difficulty, use clearly labeled ingredients or compare dough stiffness with flour/water proportions.

4.1 • Adjusting a factor

One quarter of 20 is 5. If you multiply one ingredient by 4 and the other by 4, the original ratio stays constant. If you add 4 to both, it usually changes.

4.2 • Turning Green

Start with 5 mL blue and 15 mL yellow: blue:yellow = 1:3. Two batches use 10 mL blue and 30 mL yellow.

Challenge: add 20 mL of each ingredient, getting 25:35. How much extra yellow must be added to return to 1:3? Explain why 25:75 works.

4.3 • Perfect Purple Water

Another first-party example is 8 mL blue to 3 mL red. Jada makes 24 mL blue to 9 mL red; Andre makes 16 mL blue to 9 mL red. Which preserves the original color proportion? Describe the groups in a labeled discrete diagram.

Source-skill practice

Diego mixes 10 tablespoons yellow with 2 tablespoons blue. Same-shade ratios include 15 yellow to 3 blue, or 5 yellow for every 1 blue. Do not reverse the color labels.

Clare combines 2 cups blue paint with 1 gallon white. For two batches, use 4 cups blue and 2 gallons white. To predict a darker mixture, increase blue relative to white; for a lighter one, decrease that fraction. A teacher reviews real-material observations and units.

Independent Edulytics Cool-down

An orange model mixes 3 tablespoons red and 6 tablespoons yellow. How much red and yellow would be needed for 4 batches? Draw and label four identical groups. Explain why the ratio stays 1:2.

Teacher rubric: 12 tablespoons red, 24 tablespoons yellow. Check separately the labeled ingredient groups, a comparison with a changed recipe, a clear explanation of scaling (not adding equal amounts), physical mixing or tactile alternative, and units.
