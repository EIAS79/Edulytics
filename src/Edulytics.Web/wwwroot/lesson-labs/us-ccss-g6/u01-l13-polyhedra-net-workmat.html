13.1 — Is this a polyhedron?

Polygonal faces meet along whole edges, which meet at vertices. A closed 3D surface composed of flat polygons encloses a region. Curved spheres and cones and an open-top box do not satisfy this working definition.

Original practice 1: A rectangular pyramid, B many-faced closed polyhedron and D hexagonal prism qualify. C cone and E sphere do not. Explain each classification.

13.2 — A tetrahedron net has four triangular faces

Cut out or copy this independent geometric example of a triangular-pyramid net. Three triangles share complete edges with a fourth central triangle. Mark folds and test assembly with paper/tape; the original source arrangements 1 and 2 fold, but original net 3 does not.

Teacher check: four triangles are attached along three complete source-length edges; folded tabs meet without gaps or overlap, and the resulting solid has 4 faces, 6 edges, 4 vertices. A drawing of four triangles merely touching at their corners is not a valid net.

Original practice checks

A prism with octagon bases has two octagons and eight rectangular lateral faces. A square-based prism can have rectangular side faces, so Tyler's all-squares claim is incorrect.

Independent Edulytics Cool-down — not publisher 13.4

Construct and label two different 5-face polyhedra: a triangular prism (two triangles and three rectangles) and a square pyramid (one square and four triangles). Draw a net of each and explain how each net's edges pair. Then explain why equal face counts do not make the solids the same shape.

Teacher rubric: correct type and polygonal bases; all five necessary faces; valid edge-to-edge contiguous net; folding checked without overlap; correct face-edge-vertex vocabulary; a written distinction between the solids. Numeric feedback is insufficient for this task.
