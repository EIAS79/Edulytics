Defining equivalent ratios

Two positive two-term ratios are equivalent when one common multiplier maps both matching terms. Drawing groups and explaining nonexamples are teacher-assessed.

5.1 • Dots and half dots

Invent a dot-and-half-dot display with 4 full dots and 5 half dots, then repeat the whole group twice. The comparison 4:5 becomes 8:10. Show that each category was doubled, not increased by an arbitrary amount.

5.2 • Tuna casserole scale

A full recipe uses 3 cups pasta, 6 ounces tuna, 10 ounces soup, 1 cup cheese, 1½ cups onions. A half batch uses 1½, 3, 5, ½ and ¾ in their respective original units. Explain why you cannot multiply only the soup.

If a dish measures 9 by 18 inches at fixed height, base area 162 square inches. A double batch requires twice that base area: 324 square inches, not 648. You may choose several different suitable rectangular dimensions.

5.3 • Check equivalence

Input positive numbers for two ratios A:B and C:D.

Try 18:3 and 6:1, 2:7 and 10000:35000, 6:4 and 18:8, and 3:6 and 6:3. Explain each example or counterexample with a table or diagram.

Independent Edulytics Cool-down

Explain whether 8:12 and 12:18 are equivalent. Find the shared positive factor, give a non-equivalent example, and draw two complete groups.

Teacher rubric: 8:12=2:3 and12:18=2:3; multiply both original terms by 1.5. A counterexample such as 8:12 versus12:16 fails because 8×16 ≠ 12×12. Inspect the student-created diagram and complete explanation, which selected numeric Practice does not certify.
